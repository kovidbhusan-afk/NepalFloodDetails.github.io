Summary

START HERE

What we know

Measured, or stated by the agencies responsible
  • A huge flood came down the Bhote Koshi from the Tibetan side on the morning of 26 August. Instruments picked up a shudder at 8:37 am; officials were told a flood was coming at 9:05 am.
  • Warnings went out fast. Over 600,000 text messages were sent between 9:15 and 9:16 am — inside 40 minutes of that first shudder.
  • In the hills the river rose 9 metres in 30 minutes at Galchhi and 7 metres at Malekhu.
  • Down in Chitwan, the Narayani at Devghat topped out at 6.57 m at 4 pm — below the official warning line of about 7.3 m — and was back down by 6:30 pm.
  • About 20 million cubic metres of extra water went past Devghat. The whole journey took roughly 10 hours.
  • Four of the river's automatic gauges were destroyed by the flood itself. Only Galchhi survived upstream.
  • 32 bridges and about 40 km of road are gone, including the entire road from Betrawati to the border. Around 430 MW of power generation was hit.
  • Debris has now dammed the Lhende stream about 18 km above the border, holding back a new lake. Levels rose again on 30 August and fresh alerts went out.

What we're still working out

Best current reading, not settled
  • What actually collapsed. The leading explanation is a mass of ice and rock breaking loose and crashing into the Lhende stream. Scientists at ICIMOD and partner bodies in Nepal and China are still testing it. It is not confirmed.
  • Which came first. The 8:37 am shudder was first read as an earthquake that caused the collapse. Investigators now think it was the other way round — the collapse made the shaking.
  • How big the shudder was. Nepal's technical report says magnitude 4.4. The US Geological Survey figure quoted by ICIMOD is 5.2. Nobody has reconciled them.
  • The dam-and-burst sequence. That debris blocked the river and then gave way is worked out from video and from the shape of the flood, not watched directly.
  • One leg of the journey doesn't add up. The gap between the Syabrubesi and Betrawati gauges implies the wave moved at around 85 km/h. That is very fast. The Betrawati gauge may simply have failed for another reason. Treat that stretch as uncertain.

What nobody knows yet

Genuinely open — left blank, not guessed
  • How much water is behind the new dams. Satellite analysis published by the New York Times on 28 August shows two lakes now impounded upstream, both threatening rescue operations below. The lower one covers about 0.11 square kilometres. Nobody has published how deep either is — and without depth there is no volume, and without volume there is no forecast.
  • Whether it will burst, and when. No credible timing exists from any source.
  • The flow rate in cubic metres per second at any gauge. Converting a water level into a flow rate needs a station-specific formula that hasn't been published for this event.
  • How far the water spread. Emergency satellite mapping was switched on within hours, but no confirmed flood-extent map has been released.
  • How much monsoon rain added to it further downstream. Without rainfall totals, that share can't be separated out.
  • Whether climate change caused this particular event. ICIMOD is blunt: it is too early to say. Warming is reshaping Himalayan ice and frozen ground, but no study yet links it to this collapse. Anyone claiming otherwise today is ahead of the evidence.

What probably caused it

A CHAIN, NOT A SINGLE EVENT

Disasters like this are usually a chain: something in the high mountains fails, that failure blocks a river, the block gives way, and the released water becomes a flood hundreds of kilometres downstream. Each link below is marked by how confident anyone actually is about it.

What actually fell

Nepal's Department of Hydrology puts it plainly: ice collapsed from around 5,000 metres down to 3,000 metres and dammed the Lhende river near the Chinese border. Independent analysis of satellite imagery describes a slab of glacier roughly 600 metres wide letting go from the north flank of the Langtang Himal. Falling ice behaves unlike falling rock — friction melts it on the way down, the meltwater picks up sediment, and the thickened slurry travels faster than clean water could. By the time it reached the border it was a wall.

Senior Divisional Hydrologist Binod Parajuli confirmed the four monitoring stations lost: Rasuwa Bhote Koshi, Rasuwa Syabrubesi, Nuwakot Betrawati and Dhading Malekhu (Phurke). On the power side, the Nepal Electricity Authority counted about 430 MW of generating capacity hit across six hydropower facilities; CARE's assessment counts 14 hydropower and solar projects with a combined capacity near 748 MW as affected. Those measure different things — capacity knocked offline versus projects touched — and both are provisional.

One more thing worth knowing

This same stream has now flooded severely twice in fourteen months. In July 2025 a glacial lake burst on the Tibetan side of the same catchment and destroyed the Nepal–China friendship bridge at Rasuwagadhi. The bridge had only recently been rebuilt when this flood took it again. Scientists have drawn a comparison with the 2021 Rishi Ganga disaster in India, where rock and ice collapsed into a gorge and killed more than 200 people, many of them hydropower workers.

What that recurrence does not tell us is the cause. A pattern is a reason to invest in cross-border warning systems — which is exactly what researchers have been arguing for since 2025 — but it is not the same as an attribution study, and none exists for this event.

CONTEXT

Is this climate change?

The answer depends on which question you ask
We know why the ice is melting. We have been measuring it for forty years.

What nobody has calculated yet is how much that warming contributed to this one collapse, on this one slope, on this one morning. Those are two different questions, and only the second one is still open. They get treated as one question constantly, and that is what lets the argument stall.

Question 1 — Did warming push this slab off this mountain on 26 August?

Nobody knows yet. Working that out takes a particular kind of study. It measures how much more likely warming made one specific event, and it takes months of work each time. No one has done it for this collapse. ICIMOD says so plainly, and this page will not pretend otherwise.

Question 2 — Why is there less ice up there than there used to be?

Because it is getting warmer. That is not a model or a prediction. It is what thermometers and satellites have recorded for decades. No serious scientific body disputes it.

So when someone tells you the climate link is unproven, ask which question they mean. The first one is open. The second one is not.

Why one degree is the whole story

Water at 99 °C and water at 100 °C are one degree apart, and they behave completely differently. One is hot water. The other is steam. That is because 100 is a threshold, not just another number on the way up.

Ice works the same way at the other end. At −1 °C, the ice packed into the cracks of a mountainside is holding that mountain together. It is doing the job of rock. At +1 °C it is water running out of those cracks, and the rock has nothing holding it in place.

So two degrees of warming does not make a slope two per cent weaker. It can carry the slope across the line altogether.

That frozen fill has a name. Permafrost is ground that has stayed below freezing for at least two years running. In the high Himalaya it has been frozen far longer than that — in places, longer than there has been written history. The scientists who study it work in fields called geocryology and periglacial geomorphology, and the wider study of ice, snow and frozen ground is cryosphere science.

What they find is what common sense would tell you anyway. Permanent frost, five kilometres up, in the highest mountains on earth, does not start melting for no reason. Something has to be supplying the heat.

So what is actually missing? Not the reason the ice is melting — that is settled. What is missing is one calculation, about one slab of rock, on one morning. A missing calculation is not a missing cause. If someone points at that gap as a reason to doubt why Himalayan ice is disappearing, they are pointing at the wrong thing.

You can see it from the city

Long-term residents of Kathmandu describe looking north in winter and seeing less white on the skyline than they remember. That is an impression rather than a measurement, and impressions can be wrong — but in this case the satellite record points the same way. The snow persistence data above comes from twenty-three years of continuous observation, and it is measuring exactly the thing people think they are noticing: how long snow stays on the ground after it falls. Both the eye and the instrument are describing the same retreat.

ICIMOD's director general puts the trajectory bluntly: carbon emissions have already locked in an irreversible course of recurrent snow anomalies across the region. That is not a warning about the future. It is a description of a system that has already changed.

Who caused it, and who is paying

Nepal's share of global greenhouse gas emissions is somewhere between 0.03% and about 0.1% depending on the year and the accounting method. Its own climate plan calls that contribution negligible, and it has committed to net-zero by 2045 — earlier than most wealthy countries. It also ranks among the most climate-vulnerable countries on earth, and the Asian Development Bank projects it losing roughly 2.2% of annual GDP to climate impacts by 2050.

A country that did almost nothing to cause the warming is absorbing the consequences first, in a place where the consequence is a mountain falling on a village. That is the substance of the loss-and-damage argument Nepal and other vulnerable countries have made at every climate negotiation for a decade. A fund exists. It is nowhere near the scale of the problem.

Reasonable people disagree about mechanisms — how much should flow, through which institutions, whether carbon markets speed the transition or mostly let large emitters buy their way out of changing. Those arguments are live and this page will not settle them. What is not in dispute is the arithmetic underneath: the emissions came from elsewhere, and the water came down on Rasuwa.

The part Nepal can control, and hasn't. This is not only a story about other countries. The Lhende stream flooded in July 2025 and destroyed the same bridge. Researchers called then for a cross-border, automated warning system with China. Talks happened; it was never switched on. On 26 August the warning still depended on someone phoning it in — and then four of the gauges that would have tracked the wave were destroyed by the wave.

One river did this. There are three.

PROJECTIONS, CLEARLY LABELLED

What melts in the Himalayas rises in the oceans.

That is the part most coverage stops short of. The ice that came down on Rasuwa is not only Nepal's water supply — it is also, eventually, sea level. Mountain glaciers outside the polar ice sheets are one of the largest contributors to global sea-level rise, and the Hindu Kush Himalaya holds more ice than anywhere on earth outside the poles. A village in Rasuwa and a coastline on the other side of the world are connected by the same thermometer.

And everything on this page happened along a single blue line: one tributary, of one river system, in one corner of one country. Nepal has three great river systems, and every one of them rises in the same ice.

What the projections actually say

These are model outputs, not observations, and they are given here as ranges because that is how they were published. On a high-emissions path, ICIMOD's assessment puts 70–80% of current Hindu Kush Himalayan glacier volume gone by 2100. Holding warming to 1.5 °C cuts that to roughly 30%. Snow cover across the region is projected to fall by up to a quarter.

Three-quarters of the region's glaciers are smaller than half a square kilometre, and small glaciers sit at lower, warmer elevations — closer to where people actually live. They are the ones going first.

What that means downstream is not primarily more floods like this one, though there will be those too. Around 23% of the annual flow of the twelve great rivers rising in this region comes from seasonal snowmelt. Nearly two billion people live in those basins. The long-run risk is not only water arriving all at once in the wrong place; it is water not arriving at all in the dry season.

What this section is and is not. The glacier and snow figures above are measured. The 2100 numbers are projections under stated emissions scenarios, and the range between them — 30% versus 80% — is the part that is still a choice rather than a forecast. Nothing here predicts a specific future flood in a specific valley. No such prediction is possible, and anyone offering one is guessing.

Where the response stands

  • Rescue. The Nepali Army reports pulling 2,697 people out of Rasuwa. Nepal has declined offers of foreign rescue teams, saying its own security agencies can handle the operation. Many of the worst-hit villages are still reachable only by air.
  • Immediate needs. Survivors are now facing a second crisis: medicines and clean water are running short. 242 people injured in the flood were admitted to hospitals including Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital, Bir Hospital and Rasuwa District Hospital.
  • Government money moving. NPR 10 million released to Rasuwa and Nuwakot, 5 million to Dhading, 67.5 million to affected municipalities. 37 truckloads of food and seven helicopter loads of supplies dispatched. The relief fund took in NPR 273.3 million in its first six hours, and Nepal Rastra Bank has lifted transfer caps into it.
  • International. India has sent 68.5 tonnes of relief material along with technical teams, forensic experts and equipment.
  • Access. The Trishuli flood has severed the Prithvi Highway, cutting a key route into Kathmandu. Bailey bridges are being installed to replace those swept away.
  • The long tail. Only a small fraction of the recovered dead have been identified, and mass burials are being prepared. Damage to the hydropower sector alone may run into billions. Aid officials say rebuilding homes and infrastructure — and providing mental health support to survivors — has taken months to years after past floods.

Nepali organisations working on the ground

Alongside the government fund, two locally-run groups are widely cited by Nepali organisers as sustaining longer-term rescue and relief work: Hami Nepal (@haminepal_) and the Karmayog Foundation Nepal (@KarmayogFoundationNepal). Both raise directly through their own channels. As with anything else here, go to their official accounts and verify the payment details there rather than trusting a reposted graphic.

Beyond money: share verified information rather than unconfirmed reports or old donation links. Recirculating a stale link diverts help away from where it's needed, and during a fast-moving crisis that does real damage.

How this was put together

Read the methodologyHide
  1. Nothing here was calculated. Every water level, rise, threshold and time on this page is a figure published by a named organisation. No modelling, no estimating, no filling in of gaps.
  2. Two different measurements are kept apart. Galchhi (+9 m) and Malekhu (+7 m) are how much the river rose in about half an hour. Kalikhola (12.30 m) and Devghat (6.57 m) are the total height the water reached. Mixing those up would make the flood look like it grew on the way down. It didn't.
  3. The shrinking is an observation, not a theory. Devghat went from 4.76 m at 1:30 pm to 6.57 m at 4 pm — a rise of 1.81 m. That is just subtraction on two published readings. The explanation for it (wider valley, gentler slope, tributaries diluting the surge) is standard river physics, described in words. No attempt is made to say how much each factor contributed.
  4. The forecast miss is included on purpose. Officials expected about 8 m at Devghat around 3:30 pm. They got 6.57 m at 4 pm. Over by 1.4 metres, late by half an hour. That's worth knowing before trusting any forecast for what comes next.
  5. Flow rates are missing on purpose. Turning a water level into cubic metres per second needs a formula specific to each gauge. None has been published for this event. An invented number would have looked authoritative and been meaningless.
  6. Risk colours follow a stated rule. Red where there's an active official warning about the dammed lake. Orange-red where levels are rising and no gauges are working. Orange where flow is raised with at least one gauge reporting. Yellow where the peak stayed below the warning line. Whole districts are never coloured in — a district with a river running through it is not a district that floods.
  7. Where sources disagree, both are shown. The magnitude (4.4 vs 5.2), the Devghat warning line (7.0 vs 7.3 m), the death toll across four days. One figure was never quietly picked over another.
  8. Casualty numbers are kept off the graphics. They changed every day and differ between agencies. As of 30 August, published tallies run from roughly 670 to 760 confirmed dead in Nepal, with 2,400 to 3,000 missing across Nepal and Tibet. Any single number printed on a map would be wrong within hours. For current figures, go to the source.
  9. The terrain in the 3D map is constructed. No elevation data was available. The landscape is built to match the region's known structure and the river's published heights. The river's path, the gauge positions and every timestamp are real. The hills are drawn.
